APALA
Historical and Cultural Reference Dossier
The Name Behind the Model

APALA is named in remembrance of Apālā Ātreyī (अपाला आत्रेयी), a woman associated by the Vedic textual tradition with Ṛgveda 8.91.

The important historical qualification is that Apālā is not documented as a “neuroscientist,” physician, or scientist in the modern sense. Those categories would be anachronistic. What can be responsibly said is that the Vedic tradition preserves Apālā as a named female figure and associates her with a hymn concerning illness, restoration, fertility, agency, ritual action, and transformation. Modern scholarship examines the figure through the text itself and through later Vedic traditions.

The name APALA therefore represents a modern scientific project paying homage to an ancient female figure whose voice and story survived through the Vedic textual tradition.

1. Who Was Apālā?

The name is conventionally given as Apālā Ātreyī, meaning Apālā of the Ātreya/Atri lineage. Traditional sources identify her as the daughter of Atri, hence the designation Ātreyī. The Ṛgveda hymn 8.91 is traditionally assigned to her.

Apālā belongs to the corpus of female figures associated with the Ṛgvedic tradition. Later cataloguing traditions, particularly the Sarvānukramaṇī, preserve names of female seers associated with portions of the Ṛgveda. Modern scholarship, however, emphasizes that the traditional attribution of authorship must be treated carefully: some female attributions may derive from characters or imagery appearing in the hymns rather than from demonstrable historical authorship.

For Apālā specifically, the evidence is therefore best described as:

Ṛgveda 8.91 is traditionally attributed to Apālā Ātreyī; whether the historical individual Apālā herself composed the hymn cannot be established with certainty.

This distinction is important for a historically responsible model name.

2. Primary Textual Source: Ṛgveda 8.91

The central primary source associated with Apālā is:

Ṛgveda 8.91 — Indra / Apālā Ātreyī

The hymn consists of seven verses. The traditional attribution identifies Apālā Ātreyī as the ṛṣi/seer, while Indra is the deity addressed. Modern editions of the hymn likewise identify it with Apālā.

The hymn is unusual because its central voice is that of a young unmarried woman, rather than a male priestly speaker. Five of its seven verses form the direct speech of the female speaker, with the first and last verses functioning as narrative framing.

3. The Story of Apālā in Ṛgveda 8.91

The hymn begins with a young woman going toward the water.

She discovers Soma near the path and decides to take it home and prepare it for Indra.

This opening is significant because the young woman is not merely a passive recipient of divine intervention. She acts, speaks, performs a ritualized offering, and directly articulates what she wants.

The opening verse describes her finding Soma and declaring her intention to press it for Indra:

“A young woman going to the water found Soma near the path…”

The subsequent verses describe her offering Soma and addressing Indra directly. The narrative makes clear that she is attempting to establish a relationship with the deity through her own initiative.

4. Apālā and Soma

Soma occupies an important position in Vedic ritual culture.

In the Apālā narrative, however, the manner in which she handles Soma is especially unusual. The later Jaiminīya Brāhmaṇa elaborates the episode and describes Apālā chewing the Soma stalk herself. The sound of her teeth resembles the sound of ritual pressing stones, attracting Indra's attention.

This is important because formal Soma sacrifice belonged to a highly regulated ritual environment. Modern scholarship therefore treats Apālā's independent use of Soma as an extraordinary feature of the story rather than ordinary ritual practice.

5. Apālā's Requests

The most memorable section of Ṛgveda 8.91 comes when Apālā expresses three requests to Indra.

Her requests concern:

1. Her father's head

She asks for growth upon her father's head.

The traditional interpretation connects this with her father's baldness.

2. Her father's field

She asks for the field to become productive and grow.

This introduces agricultural fertility into the story.

3. Her own body

She asks for growth upon her own body.

Modern philological interpretation connects this request with the physical signs of sexual maturation and the transition toward adulthood.

Thus, the three requests simultaneously concern:

family → agriculture → self

The prayer moves outward from her father and his land and finally returns to her own body.

6. Illness and Skin

The association between Apālā and a skin condition comes particularly strongly from later Vedic interpretation, rather than from an explicit modern clinical diagnosis in the Ṛgveda itself.

The Sāyaṇa commentary preserves a traditional legend in which Apālā, daughter of Atri, suffers from a skin disease and is rejected by her husband before returning to her father's hermitage.

The later Jaiminīya Brāhmaṇa 1.220–221 develops the story further and describes Apālā as having a skin condition or problematic coloration. She desires to remove her “bad colour” and undergoes a transformative encounter with Indra.

Modern scholarship consequently interprets the Apālā narrative as a story involving:

skin → maturation → social identity → fertility → transformation.

However, it would be historically incorrect to diagnose Apālā with a specific modern dermatological disorder.

7. The Transformation

The seventh verse of Ṛgveda 8.91 describes Indra purifying Apālā three times through the apertures associated with a chariot, cart, and yoke and giving her a “sun-skinned” appearance.

A later traditional explanation expands this imagery into a dramatic transformation.

According to the traditional Sāyaṇa interpretation, Apālā is drawn through three apertures and sheds three skins. Later retellings associate the discarded skins with reptiles.

The Jaiminīya Brāhmaṇa provides another elaboration of the episode, describing successive purification and transformation of Apālā's appearance.

The important point is that transformation is the climax of the Apālā narrative.

8. The Jaiminīya Brāhmaṇa

A second important source is the Jaiminīya Brāhmaṇa, specifically 1.220–221.

This text is later than the Ṛgvedic hymn and expands the short seven-verse narrative into a prose story. It incorporates verses from Ṛgveda 8.91 into its account of Apālā.

The Jaiminīya Brāhmaṇa tells us additional details:

Apālā is identified as the daughter of Atri.
She has a skin-related problem.
She desires to eliminate her undesirable appearance.
She encounters Soma.
She chews the Soma stalk.
Indra is attracted by what he thinks is the sound of Soma-pressing stones.
Apālā speaks verses from Ṛgveda 8.91.
Indra asks what she desires.
She states her requests.
Her transformation is then described.

The relationship between the two sources is therefore:

Ṛgveda 8.91
      ↓
short poetic narrative
      ↓
Apālā speaks directly

Jaiminīya Brāhmaṇa 1.220–221
      ↓
later prose expansion
      ↓
provides narrative context and explanation
9. Apālā as a Figure of Agency

One of the most interesting aspects of the story is agency.

Apālā does not simply wait for someone else to solve her problem.

She:

finds Soma → takes action → performs an unusual ritual act → addresses Indra → states her desires → receives transformation.

Modern scholarship has specifically noted how Apālā takes matters into her own hands and bends expected ritual boundaries in pursuit of what she wants.

This makes her a compelling symbolic namesake for a modern scientific system—not because she was practicing science, but because the literary figure is associated with initiative, seeking transformation, and confronting an affliction rather than remaining passive.

10. Apālā and the Female Voice in Vedic Literature

Apālā is part of a broader group of women associated with the Ṛgvedic textual tradition.

Examples include:

Ghoṣā
Lopāmudrā
Viśvavārā
Apālā
Romaśā
Śaśvatī
and other female figures.

A scholarly study of women associated with the Ṛgveda notes that traditional lists preserve numerous female seer attributions, although these figures vary considerably in historical status: some appear to be human women, while others are mythological or personified entities.

The existence of this tradition matters because it demonstrates that female voices are genuinely preserved within the textual corpus, even though the precise historical identities and authorship of individual hymns remain subjects of scholarly debate.

11. What We Know — and What We Do Not Know

This distinction should be preserved in any serious documentation of APALA.

Statement	Historical status

She is traditionally described as the daughter of Atri	Traditional textual attribution

The hymn contains a female first-person speaker	Textual fact

Modern Vedic scholarship is particularly cautious here. Stephanie Jamison and Joel Brereton note that traditional female author attributions in the Ṛgveda do not automatically establish female historical authorship; Carmen Spiers similarly describes Apālā's attribution as traditional while discussing the uncertainty surrounding who actually composed the hymn.

12. Why Apālā Matters Historically

Apālā is important because her story survives at the intersection of several themes:

Female speech

She is represented as speaking directly.

Ritual action

She initiates an unusual Soma offering.

Illness and the body

The later tradition connects her with a skin affliction.

Transformation

The story culminates in purification and changed appearance.

Fertility

Her requests include the productivity of land and bodily maturation.

Individual agency

The story depicts a young woman actively pursuing a desired change.

These themes have made Apālā a subject of modern philological and literary scholarship. A 2025 scholarly study by Carmen Spiers specifically examines Ṛgveda 8.91 and Jaiminīya Brāhmaṇa 1.220–221 as complementary versions of the Apālā story.

19. Historical Interpretation in One Paragraph

Apālā Ātreyī is a female figure preserved in the Vedic literary tradition and traditionally associated with Ṛgveda 8.91. In the hymn, a young woman discovers Soma, addresses Indra, performs an unusual private offering, and expresses three requests involving her father's head, the fertility of the land, and her own body. The final verse describes her purification and transformation. Later Vedic literature, especially Jaiminīya Brāhmaṇa 1.220–221, expands the story and connects her with a skin affliction and a desire for changed appearance. The identity of Apālā as a historical individual and the certainty of her authorship of the hymn remain matters of scholarly debate. Nevertheless, Apālā's textual representation as a woman confronting affliction, exercising agency, speaking directly, and seeking transformation provides a meaningful cultural inspiration for the modern APALA neurological-AI project.
